How to Save Photos and Important Documents After Water Damage: South Windsor, CT

Act within a day or two. Separate wet items gently, keep them cool, freeze what you cannot dry right away, and air dry the rest flat. Freezing stops mold and gives you time. Do not use heat or try to peel stuck pages apart.

By the South Windsor Water Damage Pros team · Updated November 12, 2026

First steps in the first 24 to 48 hours

  1. Make sure the area is safe before you go in, especially after sewage or flood water.
  2. Handle items by the edges with clean hands or gloves.
  3. Move wet items to a clean dry area and keep them out of the sun.
  4. Do not use heat, a hair dryer or an oven.
  5. Do not peel stuck pages or photos apart while wet.
  6. If you cannot dry items within a day or two, freeze them in a sealed bag.

Why freezing helps

A freezer stops mold and holds the item in place until you can deal with it. Wrap items in freezer paper, bag them and freeze. Frozen items can be thawed and dried later or sent to a document restoration service.

Do not freeze items that have been in sewage or flood water until they have been rinsed and the safety question is settled. Ask a professional about those.

Make copies and rebuild your records

  • Photograph each damaged document before it dries.
  • Request replacements for vital records, such as birth and marriage certificates.
  • Ask banks and insurers for replacement statements and policies.
  • Scan important papers and store copies in the cloud after the loss.
  • Keep a home inventory with photos for future claims.

South Windsor note: records and your insurer

The Connecticut Insurance Department points homeowners to a Home Inventory Checklist and advises documenting belongings with photos before cleanup. A current inventory also makes it easier to file a claim for contents.

Wood Memorial Library and Museum on Main Street in South Windsor is a local resource for local history questions. For vital records and replacements, ask the town clerk and the relevant state office. Call before you travel to confirm what they need.

When to call a professional

Large volumes, mold, contaminated water or irreplaceable items belong with a document restoration service. A restoration crew can pack out contents and store them while the structure dries.

Water that is still spreading comes first. See water extraction and structural drying. For flood or sewage, see flood damage cleanup. Homes in Windsor Locks and Broad Brook follow the same steps.

Prevention for next time

  • Store vital papers above floor level and away from exterior walls.
  • Use waterproof, fire-rated containers for originals.
  • Keep digital backups in a second location.
  • Avoid storing family photos in basements and garages.
  • Label boxes so you can find what matters first.

A step-by-step drying method for photographs

  1. Work in a clean area with good light and a flat surface.
  2. Fill a tray with clean cool water and gently rinse each photo to remove mud.
  3. Do not rub the image side.
  4. Lay each photo face up on paper towel or clean blotting paper.
  5. Change the paper when it is soaked.
  6. Keep photos out of direct sun and away from heat.
  7. When fully dry, store them flat in acid-free sleeves.

If many photos are stuck together, do not force them apart. Soak the stack gently in clean water and let them separate on their own, or freeze the stack and ask a conservator for help.

Digital copies are your safety net

After you rescue what you can, scan or photograph your important papers and photos. Store copies in a second place, such as a cloud service or a drive kept at a relative's home.

A scan can also show you how much text or image detail remains, which helps you decide whether to pay for professional restoration.

Items to ask a professional about

  • Oil paintings and fine art.
  • Old or rare books.
  • Photographs with unusual processes or fragile surfaces.
  • Legal documents you cannot replace.
  • Media or storage devices with irreplaceable data.

Special cases

Muddy or contaminated items

Photos and papers that touched sewage or flood water carry health risks. Wear gloves, rinse gently and wash your hands. If the item is irreplaceable, ask a conservator or document restoration service for help before you handle it more.

Wet books

Stand books on their tops with the pages fanned, in a room with moving air. Do not close a wet book. If they are valuable, freeze them in sealed bags until you can get help.

Inked or printed pages

Ink can run when wet. Do not rub or blot the surface. Lay the page flat on clean absorbent paper and let it dry slowly.

Labeling and sorting what you save

As you rescue items, sort them by type and urgency. Photographs and legal papers come first. Books and loose papers come next. Label each bag or tray with the room, the date and a short description.

Labels save time later when you ask a conservator for help or file an insurance claim. They also keep family photos from being mixed with unrelated papers. Take a photo of each labeled tray before you freeze or store it.

The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard covers drying structures. Paper and photo recovery is a separate specialty.

Photo and document questions

Can wet photographs be saved?

Often yes if you act within a day or two. Rinse gently, air dry face up or freeze them. Do not let them dry stuck together.

Should I use a hair dryer on documents?

No. Heat can warp paper and set stains. Air dry flat or freeze.

Can a wet hard drive be saved?

Do not power it on. A data recovery professional may help, and speed matters.

Is there a local library resource for South Windsor history?

Wood Memorial Library and Museum is on Main Street. Call to ask about hours and services before you visit.

Does the 24/7 line restore documents?

No. The line covers water damage and drying. Ask a document restoration service about irreplaceable items.
